What 135 Million FDA Drug Reports Actually Show

The patterns say more about how people report than about which drugs are dangerous.

Of the five things people report to the FDA most often about their medications, two of them are not side effects at all.

The single most-reported event in the entire database is "drug ineffective." Not nausea, not dizziness, not anything you would find printed on a warning label. Just: it did not work. It shows up more than 9.5 million times, and it lands in the top three reported events for over half of every medication we track — 591 of 1,163 drugs, to be exact. The fourth most-reported event, "off label use," is not a reaction either. It is simply a note that a drug was used for something other than its approved purpose.

We went through the reports behind all 1,163 medications on PillSignal, more than 135 million in total, to see what the data looks like when you zoom all the way out. The big picture is less a catalog of dangers than a portrait of how people interact with their medications, and of the reporting system that collects all of it. Here is what stood out.

The most-reported events are surprisingly ordinary

Here are the ten most-reported events across the entire dataset:

  1. Drug ineffective (9.6 million)
  2. Fatigue (8.9 million)
  3. Nausea (8.0 million)
  4. Off label use (7.7 million)
  5. Pain (7.2 million)
  6. Dyspnoea, meaning shortness of breath (6.9 million)
  7. Diarrhoea (6.9 million)
  8. Headache (6.6 million)
  9. Vomiting (5.4 million)
  10. Arthralgia, meaning joint pain (5.2 million)

Notice what is not there. No rare, frightening, headline-grabbing reactions. The leaders are the most common human complaints there are: being tired, feeling sick, hurting somewhere. These are the things people notice and bother to report, and they are also things that happen to people for a thousand reasons that have nothing to do with any medication.

That is the first thing to understand about this data. FAERS, the FDA's Adverse Event Reporting System, is a record of everything someone observed while taking a medication. It mixes genuine reactions, coincidences, the underlying illness the drug was meant to treat, and plain disappointment that a drug did not work — all into one pile. The fact that "drug ineffective" tops the list is the clearest possible proof: the database is a collection of complaints and observations, not a vetted list of a drug's effects.

The 2004 cliff, and why a big number never means a dangerous drug

Key finding: The most dramatic change in this entire dataset — a thousandfold spike in reports — happened in a single year and had nothing to do with medication safety. It was a change in paperwork.

Across the fifteen years from 1989 through 2003, the entire database holds fewer than 2,000 reports. Then, in 2004 alone, it jumped to roughly 1.5 million. In a single year, reporting went up by a factor of nearly a thousand.

No wave of newly dangerous drugs caused that. What happened in 2004 was administrative: the FDA moved toward mandatory electronic submission, and reports that had trickled in on paper suddenly poured in digitally. From there, volume kept climbing — roughly sevenfold — to a peak of about 10.7 million reports in 2022, where it has roughly plateaued since.

Sit with what that means. The most dramatic change in this entire dataset had nothing to do with medication safety and everything to do with paperwork. This is the trap nearly everyone falls into when they look at adverse event data: they see a drug with a huge number and assume it must be dangerous. But report counts track the reporting system — how many people take a drug, how long it has been on the market, and how much attention it is getting — far more than they track real-world risk.

Our own data shows this in miniature. More than a dozen aspirin brand variants each sit at roughly 500,000 reports, all bumping against the same ceiling, because aspirin is sold under countless names and taken by tens of millions of people. The reports get split across "Bayer," "Ecotrin," "Equate Aspirin," and a dozen others. None of that says anything about aspirin compared to a less common drug. It says aspirin is everywhere. Humira, one of the most widely prescribed biologics in the world, tops our dataset at 699,271 reports — largely because of how many patients take it, not because it is uniquely dangerous. This is why, across PillSignal, we report counts and never rates, and never rank drugs against each other. The numbers simply do not support that kind of comparison.

Who is doing the reporting

When demographic information is included, a clear pattern emerges. Of the roughly 123 million reports that recorded a patient's sex, 59 percent were female and 41 percent male.

Age skews older still. People aged 50 and above account for about 70 percent of all age-tagged reports, with the 50-to-64 group the single largest at roughly 28.5 percent, followed closely by the 65-to-74 group at 22.4 percent.

It is tempting to read these splits as a statement about who a medication affects. It is not. They mostly reflect who takes medications and who files reports. Older adults take more drugs, see doctors more often, and have more medical encounters where an adverse event might be recorded — so they generate more reports. The female skew has been observed in FAERS for years and likely reflects a mix of prescribing patterns and reporting behavior. These numbers describe the reporters, not the risk.

How serious the reports are, and why that word needs care

Each report can be tagged with one or more outcomes, using categories the FDA defines — not us. Across roughly 176 million outcome tags:

  1. About 35.8 percent are "other serious"
  2. About 27.6 percent involve hospitalization
  3. About 20.7 percent are non-serious
  4. About 8.7 percent record a death
  5. The remainder are life-threatening or disability outcomes

A large share of reports fall into "serious" buckets, but it is worth knowing that "serious," in FDA terms, is a broad regulatory category that captures any outcome involving hospitalization, significant disability, or similar thresholds. It is not a judgment about a specific drug.

The death figure deserves the most care of all. When a report records a death, it means a death was reported alongside a medication. It does not mean the medication caused it. Many reports involve gravely ill patients taking several drugs at once, where the underlying illness is the obvious and likely cause. A report is a flag for the FDA to investigate, not a finding of fault. Reading these counts as a measure of how deadly a drug is would be a serious mistake — and the kind of mistake this whole piece exists to prevent.

What to actually do with this

Zoomed all the way out, the lesson of 135 million reports is humbling: this data is far better at describing how people report than at telling you whether a given medication is safe for you. The biggest patterns in it — the 2004 cliff, the aspirin pile-up, the older and more female reporter base — are artifacts of how the system works, not signals about which drugs to fear.

That does not make the data useless. It makes it a starting point for better questions. If you look up your medication on PillSignal and see a reaction reported often, that is not a reason to stop taking anything. It is a reason to ask someone who knows your full medical history a specific, well-informed question. "I saw that a lot of people reported this with my medication — is that something I should watch for given my situation?" is a far better conversation than a quiet, frightened decision made alone.
